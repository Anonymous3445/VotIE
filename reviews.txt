Review 1:

Paper Summary:
This paper introduces VotIE, a new information extraction task and benchmark for extracting voting-event
arguments from municipal meeting minutes. The motivation is that municipal deliberative records encode voting
decisions in heterogeneous narrative prose, unlike parliamentary proceedings where votes are often more
standardized and structurally marked.
The task is formulated as span-level event-argument extraction. Given a segment from municipal minutes, the
system must identify textual spans corresponding to voting-related arguments, including the subject under
deliberation, voting action, voter roles such as favour/against/abstention/absent, counting expressions such as
unanimity or majority, explicit vote counts, and voting method. The benchmark is built on the CitiLink-Minutes
corpus, covering Portuguese municipal records from six municipalities.
The paper evaluates discriminative sequence labelling models and generative LLM-based extraction. The
discriminative models include a feature-based CRF, BiLSTM-CRF, and Transformer encoders with linear or CRF
heads, including BERTimbau, mDeBERTa, and XLM-R. The generative models include Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT 5.5,
and AMALIA 8B in zero-shot and five-shot settings. The main findings are that XLM-R-CRF performs best in the
standard in-domain benchmark, reaching 93.2 exact-match macro F1, while DeBERTa-CRF generalizes best
among encoders under leave-one-municipality-out evaluation. Gemini is less competitive in-domain under exact
span matching, but is more robust in the cross-municipality setting. The authors release benchmark splits, trained
models, evaluation code, and a demo.
Summary Of Strengths:
1. Municipal minutes are important for local democratic transparency, but they are difficult to process
automatically because voting outcomes are embedded in variable administrative prose.
2. Voting-event extraction from municipal records is a relevant and practical problem, especially for
journalists, civic-tech organizations, and researchers studying local governance.
3. The argument types in the schema capture key elements needed to reconstruct voting decisions:
subject, voting act, voter roles, stance, counting expressions, numeric counts, and voting method.
4. The paper gives a practical picture of when fine-tuned encoders remain preferable to LLMs for exact
span extraction, especially under strict legal/administrative traceability requirements.
5. Leave-one-municipality-out evaluation directly tests robustness to administrative writing-style variation,
which is highly relevant for real deployment.
Summary Of Weaknesses:
1. The task is framed as event extraction, but the benchmark evaluates only flat span identification. The
paper uses event-argument terminology, but does not evaluate argument-to-trigger linking, event
grouping, or role attachment. This is understandable as a first step, but it limits the claim that the
benchmark captures structured voting-event extraction. In multi-vote segments, correctly extracting all
spans is not sufficient to know which voters, counts, and subjects belong to which voting event.
2. The generative model comparison may underestimate LLM performance. The authors intentionally avoid
complex prompting, but the conclusion that LLMs underperform fine-tuned encoders for this task may
depend exactly on the chosen prompting, chunking, and exact realignment pipeline. Since generated
spans that cannot be exactly aligned are discarded, LLMs may be penalized for small paraphrases or
normalization errors even if they identify the correct semantic region.
3. The exact-match evaluation may overemphasize boundary errors relative to downstream utility. Exact
span boundaries matter for traceability, but in many civic applications, extracting the correct subject or
voter identity approximately may still be useful. The relaxed-match results partly address this, but the
paper’s main conclusions could better distinguish legal traceability from information access or search
use cases.
4. Cost comparisons between encoders and LLMs are useful but incomplete. The paper reports GPU
hours for encoders and AMALIA, while Gemini and GPT are API-based and no GPU costs are
measured. Since the conclusion emphasizes the practicality of lightweight encoders, it would be useful
to report API cost estimates, latency, and throughput for Gemini/GPT as well.
5. The benchmark is relatively small and geographically limited, with 2,885 segments from six Portuguese
municipalities and very low support for some secret-ballot/count categories; however, given that this is a
short paper introducing a first benchmark for a new task, I see this more as a natural limitation of the
contribution than as a serious flaw, provided the authors are cautious in generalizing claims and
interpreting macro-F1 for rare labels.
Comments Suggestions And Typos:
Please clarify the distinction between event-argument extraction and full event extraction. The task is valuable,
but it currently evaluates flat span identification rather than event reconstruction.
Please provide more small detail on the exact LLM prompting and realignment failure rate. How many generated
spans were discarded because they could not be matched exactly to the input?
Please include API cost or latency estimates for Gemini and GPT. The computational budget section reports
AMALIA inference time and encoder GPU hours, but the strongest generative models are API-based.
Some wording and formatting issues:
● “with he same document” should be “within the same document.”
● “the lengthier among the extracted classes” should be “the longest among the extracted classes.”
● “high inference costs makes” should be “high inference costs make.”
● “in Portugues” should be “in Portuguese.”
● “multilingual sentence-BERT embeddings show a much smaller gap” could use a little more explanation.
● The notation alternates between VOTER-AGST, VOTER-AGAINST, V-AGN; please ensure consistency
or define abbreviations clearly.
● “macro averaged” should be “macro-averaged.”
● The paper says the schema has twelve argument types, but macro averages are over eleven because
Count-Against has zero test instances; this should be made very explicit in the main results.

Paper Summary:
This paper introduces VotIE (Voting Information Extraction), a novel task and benchmark designed to extract structured voting-event arguments from narrative municipal meeting minutes. Experimental results show that fine-tuned encoders, specifically XLM-R-CRF, achieve the strongest exact-match performance in standard in-domain evaluations, significantly outperforming generative approaches.
Summary Of Strengths:
The paper introduces VotIE, the first benchmark for structured extraction of voting events from heterogeneous narrative municipal minutes.
Summary Of Weaknesses:
The benchmark is limited to flat span identification and does not yet address deliberative event reconstruction, argument–trigger linking, or cross-segment coreference resolution.
Comments Suggestions And Typos:
Despite the zero-shot adaptability of generative models, their high computational costs currently constrain their practicality, making lightweight fine-tuned encoders the more viable option for large-scale deployments.
The placement of the appendix appears to be inconsistent with the standard paper format. Typically, the References section should follow the Conclusion and Ethical Considerations, with the Appendix placed at the end of the manuscript.

REVIEW 3:

Paper Summary:
This paper introduce VotIE(Voting Information Extraction), a task for extracting voting-event arguments from municipal meeting minutes. The author define a schema of 5 categories of voting event arguments and build a benchmark with 120 documents from 6 municipalities based on the existing CitiLink-Minutes corpus. They evaluate both discriminative models and generative LLMs under in-domain and cross-municipality settings. The results show that fine-tuned XLM-R-CRF achieves the best in-domain performance, while Gemini shows better robustness in the cross-municipality setting.
Summary Of Strengths:
Comprehensive baseline comparison. The paper covers a good range of models across two paradigms: feature-based CRF, BiLSTM-CRF, three Transformer encoders (with both linear and CRF heads), and three generative LLMs. This gives a relatively complete picture of the current technical landscape for this task.
Cross-municipality evaluation is a good design choice. The LOMO protocol directly tests generalization to unseen administrative writing styles, which is the core practical challenge for this domain.
Thorough limitations section. The paper clearly discusses the gap between span identification and full event extraction, the coreference issue, dataset limitations, and language-specific concerns.
Summary Of Weaknesses:
The task frame is misleading. The paper repeatedly frames VotIE as "event-argument extraction" and connects it to Event Extraction literature. However, as the authors acknowledge in Section 8, the actual evaluation is flat span identification. There is no argument-to-trigger binding, no event grouping. The gap between the frame and the actual task is significant. While the authors justify this as a first benchmarking step, the paper should present honestly as what it actually evaluates: a sequence labeling task for voting-related entity extraction. The current frame overstates the contribution.
Limited dataset contribution. The annotations come entirely from the CitiLink-Minutes corpus. This paper's contribution is defining the splits and evaluation protocol, not creating the annotations. This should be made clearer. Also, the dataset is quite small: only 120 documents, 2885 segments and 6 municipalities. Several entity types have very low support in the test set (Count-Favor: 4, Count-Blank: 5, Voting-Method: 5). The evaluation on these types is essentially meaningless. For example, in Table 11, many models score 100% on types with only 4-5 test instances.
Writing quality needs improvement. Many sentences are unnecessarily long and complex, making the paper hard to follow. For example: "Figure 1 illustrates the coupling that motivates the schema: outcome gains procedural meaning only in relation to the accompanying Voter-Abstention and Voter-Against spans that define who falls outside the majority, requiring joint interpretation of procedural voting language rather than recognition of isolated lexical mentions." (lines 151-158) — this is very difficult to understand.
Single-language limitation weakens generalization claims. The entire study is on Portuguese municipal minutes. However, it is claimed to be theoretically language-agnostic(Section 8, line 402).
The appendices A-D are placed before the Reference section. This does not follow the required format.
Comments Suggestions And Typos:
"Reference" section should appear before the appendices. The ordering of the sections should be checked and fixed.
The error analysis (Section 6, Table 3) is useful but brief. It would be more informative to show some concrete error examples.
Section 3: The formal definition S = {(sj, ej, tj)} uses character offsets, but the BIO-based models operate on token-level. The mismatch between the formal definition and the actual implementation should be discussed more clearly.
Several paragraphs or sections end without a full stop. For example, the end of the "Extraction Paradigms" paragraph (line 214), the end of the "Dataset limitations" paragraph (line 398), and the end of Section B.5 (line 653).

REVIEW 4:

Paper Summary:
This paper introduces VotIE (Voting Information Extraction). It is a new task for extracting structured voting event arguments from municipal meeting minutes at the span level. The authors build a benchmark using the existing CitiLink-Minutes corpus. They conduct comprehensive experiments comparing 8 models. These include sequence labeling architectures and generative large language models. The study evaluates these models in both standard in-domain and Leave-One-Municipality-Out (LOMO) settings.

Summary Of Strengths:
Clear Task Value: The task definition is clear and highly practical. Municipal minutes are vital for public oversight but exist as unstructured narratives. VotIE successfully addresses the real-world challenge of processing these administrative texts.
Insightful Evaluation Design: The cross-municipality LOMO evaluation is an excellent contribution. Many domain-specific extraction papers ignore out-of-domain generalization. The LOMO experiments highlight the generalization advantages of LLMs. This provides highly useful guidance for deploying extraction systems in the real world.
Summary Of Weaknesses:
Limited Technical Novelty: The study does not propose new extraction methods or introduce new data. It relies entirely on the previously published CitiLink-Minutes dataset (ECIR 2026) and applies standard baseline models.

Meta-Review:

Metareview:
The submission defines a new IE task called Voting IE (VotIE). The new task is span-level identification of several pre-defined categories (or, entity types). The categories are voting-related:

the vote subject (what was voted on)
the voting result (approved or not)
votes of individuals (e.g. the BE councilor voted against, where BE councilor is some municipal role)
vote aggregation (e.g. by majority or unanimously)
The annotations themselves come from an existing corpus (CitiLink-Minutes) which annotated meeting minutes over free form Portuguese text from six municipalities. The task is defined with a specific subset of all categories defined in the CitiLink-Minutes corpus, where the subset is selected to be voting related categories.

The submission establishes benchmark results for the VotIE task with several span labeling discriminative models, and LLMs. This is done in two setups. In an in-domain setup, training and evaluation are on texts from one municipality. In the cross-domain (cross-municipality) setup, training is on all domains (all municipalities) but one, and test is on the left-out domain (leave-one-municipality-out, or LOMO).

Results show that trained models are best for the in-domain setup, especially when requiring for exact span matches. When relaxing that requirement, and considering relaxed match as the metric, the gap between trained models and LLMs is significantly reduced (still in the in-domain setup realm). In the more practical LOMO setup, LLMs generally perform better, in almost all cases.

Summary Of Reasons To Publish:
Automatic identification of voting events in free form text is an important and worthy goal.
The cross-municipality evaluation setup is practical and useful.
A benchmarking experiment over Portuguese data (not common)
Selected baselines are appropriate for the task. The list of baselines is comprehensive.
Note on the suggested revisions listed below: for a short paper, a findings paper fits. The suggested revisions could be handled for the camera ready version.
Summary Of Suggested Revisions:
Clarity: the framing should be revised, specifically, describe the addressed task without any ambiguity, and its setup (span-level identification in texts). Also, describe how the defined task differs from the tasks and experiments explored in the CitiLink-Minutes paper. Is the task a subset of all categories (event types) defined in that corpus?
Error analysis: adding a manual analysis over a sample would be useful.
Focus should be more on the LOMO setup, as it is more practical (and, subjectively, more interesting). Consider adding the additional methods included in Table 1 to Table 2.
Novelty is a bit limited, while the task is new, it is a reformulation over an existing corpus, and a benchmark of contemporary methods. While the results are novel (since the task is new) the general trend fits current knowledge: having training data in a domain allows small models to beat LLMs, yet LLMs generalize better to new domains.
